Air service licence financial fitness in East Africa

An airline needs two permissions: the Air Operator Certificate, which is about safety, and the air service licence, which is the economic licence. To grant the licence, each regulator asks the applicant to prove something about its finances. This note reads that test in the licensing instrument of Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda and Ethiopia, sets the five side by side, and shows what each means for a business plan.

The five tests

What each regulator asks you to prove

Read from each jurisdiction's licensing instrument and its financial-fitness provision, in regulator-hosted or gazette copies, on 27 November 2026.

Where they agree

Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda and Rwanda use the same two-limb test: obligations met over a horizon, and a block of fixed and operating costs covered without relying on revenue. The wording closely resembles the European Union's operating-licence test (Regulation 1008/2008, Art. 5), though that text was not read for this note.

Where they differ

  • Tanzania and Uganda ask for six months of costs covered, twice Kenya's and Rwanda's three. For the same plan, the working capital the regulator expects doubles.
  • Tanzania's obligations horizon is 12 months, not 24.
  • Uganda adds published financing thresholds by scope of service.
  • Rwanda adds a capital test for small carriers.
  • Ethiopia sets no quantified test. The Proclamation leaves fitness to the Authority's judgement; the ECAA's directives and forms could not be reached.

What the figures mean for a plan

From the months to a working-capital figure

The cost-cover limb turns into a number once the plan has a revenue figure and an EBITDA margin. Annual operating cost is revenue less EBITDA, that is revenue × (1 − EBITDA margin). The working capital the test expects is that operating cost × months ÷ 12. This is the arithmetic the Greenfield Venture Builder uses.

Illustrative example, not a benchmark A plan with USD 10m of revenue at a 20% EBITDA margin has an annual operating cost of USD 8.0m. Three months of that (Kenya, Rwanda) is USD 2.0m; six months (Tanzania, Uganda) is USD 4.0m. The revenue and margin are chosen to show the arithmetic and come from no publication. Ethiopia sets no figure, so none is computed for it.

Reference frame

The regional and international texts

None of these sets a test a business plan could compute.

  • Yamoussoukro Decision, Art. 6.9. It has no financial-capacity criterion and relies on the national licence.
  • AFCAC's 2024 model economic regulations. They ask for "proof of adequate financial capability", which is discretionary, and the model text is not binding.
  • EAC Treaty, Art. 92. It places duties on States, not applicants.
  • CASSOA. Its mandate is safety and security only.
  • ICAO guidance (Docs 9587 and 9626). It could not be retrieved and is recorded as a gap.

Limits

What this note could not confirm

  • Kenya. No amendment to the 2018 Regulations since gazettal was found, but the 2022 consolidation and the 2024 draft replacement could not be checked.
  • Tanzania. Reg. 8 sits in the Part on internal services; how it is applied to an international licence is to be confirmed with the TCAA. The provision of Cap. 80 that keeps the 2006 Regulations in force was not read.
  • Uganda. The figures are in guidelines, not the Regulations. Revised draft regulations reported in 2026 were not found.
  • Rwanda. The copy read sits in RCAA's archived folder but is linked from its live service page. No change to 37.085 in 2024 was found.
  • Ethiopia. The English column was read; the Amharic text prevails. ECAA directives were unreachable.
  • ICAO. Docs 9587 and 9626 were blocked at retrieval.

This note is a planning aid, not legal advice. Confirm the current text with the regulator before an application. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by any of the authorities named.
