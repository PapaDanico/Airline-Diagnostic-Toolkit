Returns & Reverse-Solve

A capital plan usually shows revenue and capital and stops, leaving the reader to wonder whether the numbers earn anything. This turns the question round. Given the revenue and the capital, it asks what you would have to believe: how much debt the cash flow will carry at the lender's covenant, what margin a given return needs, and what the business must be worth at the end for a hurdle rate to be met. It is arithmetic on your figures, built to be argued with.

Step 5

What the business must be worth

Cash flow over five years rarely repays the capital. The rest of the return has to come from what the business is worth at the end. This is that value for your hurdle and for two either side.

Step 6

How wrong can the inputs be?

Say how far you think revenue, margin and interest could miss. The first table moves each input alone and ranks them by how much the debt capacity swings. The second moves them together, 2,000 times, with the same seed every time, and reports the cautious (P10), middle (P50) and generous (P90) outcomes.

Ranges are yours and are drawn as symmetric triangles around your figures, independently. If you have no view, the defaults are wide enough to be uncomfortable, which is the point.
